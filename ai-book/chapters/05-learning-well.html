The goal is new examples

Nobody needs a network that can sort the emails it was trained on; we already know their labels. What we want is a network that sorts tomorrow's emails correctly. Doing well on examples the network has never seen is called generalisation, and it is the real goal of all machine learning.

Underfitting and overfitting

There are two ways to fail.

  • Underfitting: the network is too simple, or hasn't trained long enough, to capture the pattern. It does badly on training data and on new data. Tiny AI with one neuron trying to separate the crescents is underfitting: a straight line can't follow a curve.
  • Overfitting: the network is so flexible, or trained so long, that it learns the training examples themselves, including their random noise and quirks, instead of the pattern underneath. It looks brilliant on training data and does badly on anything new.

Analogy

Two students prepare for a maths exam using past papers. One memorises the answers. The other learns the methods. On the past papers, both score 100%. On the real exam, with new questions, only one of them passes. An overfitted network is the student who memorised the answers.

Train, validation, test

To catch overfitting, you hide some data from the network. The data is split three ways:

  • The training set: what the network learns from.
  • The validation set: checked regularly during training, never learned from. Used to choose hyperparameters and decide when to stop.
  • The test set: locked away and used once at the very end, for an honest final score.
{{fig:overfit_curve|Training loss keeps falling, but validation loss starts rising once the network begins memorising. The best moment to stop is at the bottom of the validation curve.}}

If information from the test set sneaks into training, for example because the same email appears in both, the final score is falsely good. This is called leakage or contamination, and it is a real problem for language-model benchmarks, because the internet text they train on often contains the test questions (Chapter 17).

In your projects

Tiny AI's “Real acc.” number was a test set: fresh points, checked against the true pattern, never trained on. That is why it told the truth when the model learned from itself and collapsed, even though the model's own training data looked fine.

Regularisation: making memorising harder

Techniques that discourage overfitting are called regularisation. The common ones:

  • Early stopping: stop training when the validation loss stops improving.
  • Weight decay: gently shrink all weights towards zero at every step, so the network only keeps large weights that really earn their place. Simpler explanations are preferred.
  • Dropout: during training, randomly switch off a fraction of the neurons (say 10%) at each step. The network can't rely on any single neuron, so knowledge gets spread out and becomes more robust. At inference time, every neuron is switched back on.
  • Data augmentation: make more training data by changing the examples slightly: flip and crop photos, add noise to audio. A cat is still a cat when the picture is mirrored.
Analogy

Dropout is like a football team that trains with random players missing each day. Nobody can rely on the star striker always being there, so everyone learns to cover every position. On match day, with the full team, they are stronger and more flexible.

The best regulariser: more good data

The most reliable cure for overfitting is more data. With a million examples, it is much harder to memorise them all than it is to learn the real pattern. This is a big reason why modern models train on such vast datasets.

Quality matters as much as quantity. The old saying “garbage in, garbage out” applies strongly. If labels are wrong, the network learns the mistakes. If the data over-represents some groups and under-represents others, the network inherits that imbalance; this is one source of bias, covered in Chapter 18. Much of the real work in AI companies is collecting, cleaning, filtering and labelling data.

Keeping deep networks trainable

Two more problems appear as networks get deeper, and their fixes are in every modern model, so the names are worth knowing.

Vanishing and exploding gradients

During backprop, blame is multiplied at every layer on its way back. Multiply many numbers smaller than 1 and you get almost nothing; multiply many numbers bigger than 1 and you get a huge number. So in very deep networks, the first layers might receive almost no learning signal (vanishing gradients) or wildly large ones (exploding gradients).

Residual connections

The main fix, from 2015, is the residual connection (or skip connection). Instead of each layer replacing its input, it adds its output to its input. The original signal always has a clear path straight through the network, like an express lane, and each layer only needs to learn a small correction. This made networks with over a hundred layers trainable.

Normalisation

Normalisation layers rescale the numbers flowing through the network so they stay in a sensible range, neither tiny nor huge. Batch normalisation is common in image networks; layer normalisation (LayerNorm, or its simpler cousin RMSNorm) is used in language models. Tiny Writer has three LayerNorms.

In plain words

Learning is easy; learning the right thing is hard. Hold some data back to check honestly, stop memorising with regularisation, feed in more and better data, and use residual connections and normalisation to keep deep networks trainable.
