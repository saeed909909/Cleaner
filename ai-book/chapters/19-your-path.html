Reading it aloud: a 30-billion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model, which uses only 3 billion parameters per token so it runs fast; it has been instruction-tuned to work as an assistant; it can see 32,000 tokens at once; and this copy is quantized to about 4 bits in the GGUF format, so it needs roughly 17–18 GB of memory. If you can follow that, you can follow almost any model announcement.

Try it yourself

Next time you see a model release, try to answer: How many total and active parameters? Dense or MoE? Base or instruct? What context length? What precision is the file? Will it fit in my VRAM? Each answer is a chapter of this book.

The road to your own language model

The plan discussed for your RTX 2060 Super, restated in the book's vocabulary:

  1. Run models first. Install LM Studio or Ollama and try a few 4-bit open-weight models in the 3–8B range. It's the quickest way to get a feel for sizes, quantization levels, context and speed on your own card.
  2. Set up the stack. NVIDIA driver, Python, PyTorch with CUDA. Train something tiny to confirm the GPU is used.
  3. A small pretraining run. A decoder-only transformer of about 10 million parameters, a BPE tokenizer, a few hundred million tokens of public-domain books. FP16 mixed precision (your card has no BF16). About an hour. Watch the loss curve and the samples improve.
  4. The main run. About 124 million parameters, GPT-2-small sized, on 2–3 billion tokens, roughly following the Chinchilla rule. Several days to a couple of weeks, with checkpoints so you can stop and resume.
  5. Make it an assistant. Supervised fine-tuning on example conversations, so it answers rather than just continuing text.
  6. Ship it. Save as safetensors, convert to GGUF, quantize, and load it in LM Studio like any other model. Write a model card. Optionally publish on Hugging Face.
Good to know

Set expectations honestly: a 124M model will write fluent paragraphs that drift off topic, and won't be a reliable source of facts or reasoning. That isn't failure; it is the size GPT-2 small was. What you'll have is a real language model whose every part you understand, which very few people can say.

Where to learn more

A short, reliable reading and watching list, from gentle to deep. All are well established and free.

  • 3Blue1Brown, “Neural networks” (YouTube series): beautiful visual explanations of neurons, gradient descent, backpropagation, transformers and attention.
  • Andrej Karpathy, “Neural Networks: Zero to Hero” (YouTube) and his nanoGPT code: building GPT from scratch, line by line. The best next step for your PC project.
  • Jay Alammar, “The Illustrated Transformer” (blog post): the transformer drawn step by step.
  • Hugging Face courses (huggingface.co/learn): practical courses on using and fine-tuning models.
  • fast.ai, “Practical Deep Learning for Coders”: a hands-on course that starts with working code.
  • “Attention Is All You Need” (Vaswani et al., 2017): the original transformer paper. Short, and readable once you've finished this book.

A last word

Every system in this book, from your 100-parameter network to the largest models in the world, comes down to the same few ideas: numbers in layers, a measure of error, small steps downhill, and a great deal of data. The giant models are not a different kind of thing; they are this book's ideas multiplied by billions, plus a lot of careful engineering.

The vocabulary will keep growing. New architectures, new tricks and new acronyms arrive every month. But they will almost always be new arrangements of the parts you now know: neurons, layers, attention, experts, tokens, bits and chips. When you meet a new term, ask which of those parts it changes, and you'll usually have it.
