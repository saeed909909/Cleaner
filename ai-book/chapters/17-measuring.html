Perplexity: how surprised is the model?

The most basic measure of a language model comes straight from its training loss. Perplexity turns the cross-entropy loss into a more intuitive number: roughly, how many options the model is torn between at each step, on average.

perplexity = eloss (e ≈ 2.718)

{{fig:perplexity|Loss and perplexity are the same information on different scales. Tiny Writer starts at a loss of 4.56, which is a perplexity of 96: as unsure as picking blindly among all 96 characters.}}

Tiny Writer at a loss of 2.2 has a perplexity of about 9: as if, at each letter, it had narrowed the choice to nine equally likely options. A good letter-level model gets below 3. Perplexity is useful for comparing models with the same tokenizer on the same text, but it says nothing about whether a model is helpful, honest or good at reasoning. For that, people use benchmarks.

Benchmarks

A benchmark is a fixed set of test questions with known answers, used to compare models. Some well-known ones, as examples of the kinds of skill tested:

BenchmarkTests
MMLUMultiple-choice questions across 57 school and university subjects
GSM8K, MATHMaths word problems, from school level to competition level
HumanEvalWriting short programs that pass hidden tests
SWE-benchFixing real bugs in real open-source software projects
GPQAVery hard science questions written by PhD experts
ARC-AGIVisual puzzles that are easy for people but hard for AI
Humanity's Last ExamExtremely difficult questions from experts across many fields

Benchmarks have a short life. As models improve, scores approach 100% and the test can no longer tell good models from great ones. This is called saturation, and it is why new, harder benchmarks keep appearing. MMLU and GSM8K, which were hard in 2021, are now mostly saturated by top models.

Why scores can mislead

  • Contamination. Benchmark questions get posted online, and online text ends up in training data. A model may have seen the answers (Chapter 5's leakage, on a global scale).
  • Teaching to the test. When a number becomes the goal, effort goes into raising the number rather than the ability behind it. This is known as Goodhart's law: “when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure”.
  • Setup differences. Scores change with how questions are asked, how many examples are shown, and whether the model may think first. Companies don't always compare like with like.
  • Narrowness. A model that is top at maths may be worse at writing, or at following your particular instructions.
Analogy

School exam grades. They tell you something real, but a student who drilled past papers can outscore one who understands more, and a top grade in maths says nothing about someone's essay writing. Grades are a starting point for judging a person, not the final word.

Asking people: arenas and preferences

Another approach skips fixed answers and asks people. In a chatbot arena, a user types any question, two anonymous models answer, and the user votes for the better reply. Across millions of votes, each model gets a rating, using the same Elo rating system as chess players. This captures what people actually prefer, but people sometimes prefer answers that are longer, friendlier or more confident rather than more correct.

Evals: testing for a specific purpose

In the industry, any systematic test of a model is called an eval. Besides public benchmarks, companies build private evals for their own needs: does the model follow our formatting rules, does it refuse dangerous requests, can it complete this 50-step task? Safety evals test for risky abilities, such as helping with weapons or deceiving its operators. Anyone building with AI is advised to write a few evals of their own: a set of real examples from their use, with what a good answer looks like.

Reading a model launch chart

When a new model announces its scores, ask:

  1. Which benchmarks, and are they saturated? Gains near 100% matter less.
  2. Compared with which models, and which versions? Is anything strong missing?
  3. Same conditions? Thinking time, number of attempts, tools allowed?
  4. Do independent testers and arena ratings agree?
  5. Most importantly: how does it do on your tasks?
In plain words

Perplexity measures prediction; benchmarks measure specific skills; arenas measure what people prefer. Each is useful and each can be gamed. Trust patterns across many measures, and test on what you actually need.
