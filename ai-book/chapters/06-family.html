Why different shapes?

An MLP, where every neuron connects to every input, can in principle learn anything. In practice it is wasteful. A small photo has about a million pixel values; a single dense layer of a thousand neurons would need a billion weights, and it would have to learn separately that a cat in the top-left corner and a cat in the bottom-right are the same thing.

So researchers design networks whose wiring matches the shape of the data. The design of a network, meaning which layers it has and how they connect, is called its architecture. This chapter is a tour of the important ones.

Convolutional networks: for images

A convolutional neural network (CNN) is built around a small window, called a kernel or filter, typically 3 × 3 pixels. The same small set of weights slides across the whole image, step by step, checking every position for one pattern, such as a vertical edge. The result is a new image, a feature map, which is bright wherever the pattern was found. This sliding operation is called a convolution.

{{fig:conv|A 3 × 3 kernel slides across the image. At each position it multiplies the 9 pixels under it by its 9 weights and adds them up, producing one value in the feature map. The same 9 weights are reused everywhere.}}
Analogy

Imagine searching a huge map for every church using a small cardboard stencil with a church shape cut out. You slide the stencil over the map square by square, and mark each place where it matches. You don't need a separate stencil for every location: one stencil, reused everywhere. A CNN layer is a stack of dozens of different stencils, each looking for its own pattern.

Because the same few weights are reused everywhere, a convolution layer needs very few parameters, and it recognises a pattern wherever it appears. CNNs also use pooling layers, which shrink the feature maps (for example keeping only the strongest value in each 2 × 2 square), so later layers see a zoomed-out view. Stack convolutions and pooling, and you get the edges → parts → objects hierarchy from Chapter 3.

Good to know

In 2012 a CNN called AlexNet won the ImageNet competition, sorting photos into 1,000 categories with far fewer errors than any previous method. It was trained on two gaming graphics cards. This is usually taken as the start of the deep-learning boom.

Recurrent networks: for sequences

Text, speech and stock prices come in sequences, where order matters. A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads a sequence one item at a time. Alongside each new item, it receives its own output from the previous step, a running summary called the hidden state. The network carries this memory forward as it reads.

{{fig:rnn|An RNN “unrolled” over time. The same network (the same weights) reads one word per step and passes its hidden state, its memory, to the next step.}}
Analogy

An RNN reads like a person with a small notepad who may only look at one word at a time. After each word they update their notes, then the word is gone forever. By the end of a long chapter, details from page one have been written over many times.

That last point is the RNN's weakness: it struggles to remember things from far back. The long short-term memory network (LSTM, 1997) added gates, small learned switches that decide what to write into memory, what to keep, and what to erase. LSTMs, and a simpler cousin called the GRU, powered speech recognition and translation until about 2017. But they still read one step at a time, which makes them slow to train on modern hardware. Transformers replaced them.

Transformers: read everything at once

The transformer, introduced in 2017, drops the step-by-step reading. It looks at the whole sequence at once and lets every word directly check every other word for relevance, using a mechanism called attention. That makes it both better at long-range connections and much faster to train on GPUs, because all positions are processed in parallel. Almost every modern language model, and many image and audio models, is a transformer. Chapter 9 is entirely about it.

Autoencoders: squeeze and rebuild

An autoencoder is trained to copy its input to its output, through a narrow bottleneck in the middle. The first half, the encoder, must squeeze the input into a few numbers; the second half, the decoder, must rebuild the original from them. To succeed, the bottleneck has to capture what really matters about the input. The compressed description is called the latent representation, and the space of all such descriptions is the latent space.

{{fig:autoencoder|An autoencoder: the encoder compresses the input into a small latent code, and the decoder rebuilds the input from it.}}
Analogy

Describing a friend's face over the phone so someone can sketch it. You can't send every detail, so you pick what matters: round face, big eyebrows, short curly hair. A good description in a few words is a good latent code.

The words encoder and decoder come back in language models: some models are encoder-only (good at understanding text), some decoder-only (good at writing it), and GPT-style chat models are decoder-only.

GANs: the forger and the detective

A generative adversarial network (GAN, 2014) trains two networks against each other. The generator creates fake images from random noise. The discriminator is shown a mix of real and fake images and must spot the fakes. Each gets better by beating the other.

{{fig:gan|A GAN: the generator tries to fool the discriminator; the discriminator tries to catch it. Both learn from the result.}}
Analogy

An art forger and a detective. Every time the detective spots a fake, the forger studies what gave it away. Every time a fake slips through, the detective studies what they missed. After thousands of rounds, the forgeries are superb.

GANs produced the first startlingly real fake faces around 2018. They are hard to train, because the two networks can fall out of balance, and for most image generation they have been overtaken by diffusion models.

Diffusion models: sculpting from noise

A diffusion model learns to remove noise. During training, real images are blurred with more and more random static until nothing is left, and the network learns to undo one step of that damage at a time. To create a new image, you start from pure static and ask the network to “clean it up” again and again. An image appears out of the noise.

{{fig:diffusion|Diffusion. Training adds noise step by step (left to right); generating runs the process backwards, removing a little noise at each step, starting from pure static.}}
Analogy

A sculptor who says the statue was always inside the marble and they just removed what didn't belong. The diffusion model starts with a block of random noise and chips away, step by step, guided by your text description, until an image is left.

Most popular image and video generators use diffusion, usually with a text description steering each step (Chapter 13).

Graph networks: for connections

Some data is neither a grid nor a sequence but a web of connections: molecules (atoms joined by bonds), road maps, social networks. A graph neural network (GNN) lets each item pass messages to its neighbours, round after round, so each one learns from its surroundings. GNNs are used in drug discovery, weather forecasting and recommendation systems.
