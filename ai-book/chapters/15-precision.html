Bits and bytes

Computers store everything as bits, each a 0 or a 1. Eight bits make a byte. A number stored in 32 bits takes 4 bytes; in 16 bits, 2 bytes; in 8 bits, 1 byte; in 4 bits, half a byte. Since a model is mostly billions of weights, the number of bits per weight decides its size:

model size in bytes ≈ number of parameters × bits per weight ÷ 8

So the same 7B model needs 28 GB in its original 32-bit form (no chance on an 8 GB card) but only about 3.5 GB at 4 bits (fits easily). Understanding how that shrinking works is the subject of this chapter.

Floating-point numbers

Weights are usually stored as floating-point numbers, a computer version of scientific notation. A number like 0.000314 is stored as 3.14 × 10−4: a sign (plus or minus), an exponent (how big or small: the power of ten, or in computers, of two), and a mantissa (the significant digits: 3.14). More exponent bits give a wider range of sizes; more mantissa bits give finer precision.

{{fig:bits|How the bits are shared out in common number formats. FP32 has plenty of both range and precision. FP16 keeps precision but loses range. BF16 keeps FP32's range but loses precision. Integer formats have no exponent at all.}}
  • FP32 (“single precision”): 32 bits. The traditional default. Very accurate, but big and slow.
  • FP16 (“half precision”): 16 bits. Half the size, much faster on tensor cores. Its weakness is range: very small numbers, like tiny gradients, can round to zero.
  • BF16 (“brain float”, invented at Google Brain): also 16 bits, but with the same exponent range as FP32 and less precision. Neural networks cope well with imprecision but badly with numbers falling out of range, so BF16 became the standard for training. Your RTX 2060 Super's tensor cores do not support it, so on that card you would train in FP16.
  • FP8 and even FP4: used on the newest data-centre GPUs for very fast training and inference.
  • INT8 and INT4: whole numbers from −128 to 127, or −8 to 7, with no exponent, combined with a scale factor. Used for compressed models.
Analogy

Writing down distances. FP32 is writing “1,234.5678 km”. BF16 is “about 1,200 km”: rough, but you can still write anything from the width of an atom to the distance to the Sun. FP16 is careful to the metre, but can't write anything bigger than a few dozen kilometres. For a neural network, being roughly right about everything beats being exactly right about a narrow range.

Modern training uses mixed precision: the heavy matrix maths runs in 16 bits (or 8) for speed, while a master copy of the weights and some sensitive sums are kept in 32 bits so that tiny updates aren't lost.

Quantization: shrinking a trained model

Quantization converts a trained model's weights to fewer bits, for example from 16-bit to 4-bit. Each weight is rounded to the nearest of a small set of allowed values, and a scale factor is stored alongside each small group of weights so the values can be stretched back to roughly the right size.

{{fig:quant|Quantization. Each original weight (dots) is rounded to the nearest of a few allowed levels (lines). With 4 bits there are only 16 levels, so some precision is lost, but each weight takes a quarter of the space of a 16-bit one.}}
Analogy

Saving a photo with fewer colours. A photo with millions of colours, reduced to 256, looks almost identical. Reduced to 16 colours, you start to see banding in the sky, but the picture is still clearly recognisable, and the file is far smaller. Reduce to 2 colours and it falls apart.

How much quality is lost?

  • 8-bit: usually almost indistinguishable from the original.
  • 4- to 6-bit: a small, often hard-to-notice loss for most uses. This is the popular sweet spot for running models at home.
  • 2- to 3-bit: noticeable damage, especially for smaller models; large models tolerate it better.

A useful rule: a bigger model at 4 bits usually beats a smaller model at 16 bits that takes the same memory.

Quantization is mainly for running models. Clever methods, with names like GPTQ and AWQ, use a little sample text (calibration data) to choose the rounding so the important weights lose the least. In the popular GGUF file format used by llama.cpp, Ollama and LM Studio, you'll see quantization levels with names like Q8_0, Q5_K_M or Q4_K_M: the number is roughly the bits per weight, and the letters describe the method and mix.

Will it fit? The memory budget

Running a model needs memory for more than just the weights:

  1. The weights: parameters × bytes per weight.
  2. The KV cache (Chapter 11): grows with the context length. For a 7B-class model, a few thousand tokens of context might take a few hundred megabytes; a hundred thousand tokens can take many gigabytes.
  3. Working space and overhead: the software itself, temporary results, and on Windows, your screen. Budget about 1 GB.
{{fig:vram_budget|What fits in 8 GB of VRAM (approximately). Each bar shows the weights plus a modest KV cache and overhead; the dashed line is your card's limit.}}
In your projects

On your 8 GB card: a 7–8B model at 4-bit runs comfortably with a few thousand tokens of context. A 12–14B model at 4-bit is right at the limit, and usually needs a few layers kept in system RAM, which slows it down. At 16-bit, about 3B is the largest that fits. And for the training project, a 124M model is tiny by comparison: about 0.25 GB of weights, leaving plenty of room.

Training needs much more

Training is far hungrier than running. For each parameter, mixed-precision training with Adam typically stores the 16-bit weight, a 32-bit master copy, a 16-bit gradient, and Adam's two 32-bit memory values: around 16 bytes per parameter, before counting the activations (the intermediate results from the forward pass, kept for backprop).

ModelRunning, 4-bitRunning, 16-bitTraining (≈16 bytes/param)
124M0.06 GB0.25 GB2 GB + activations
1B0.5 GB2 GB16 GB + activations
7B3.5 GB14 GB112 GB + activations

That's why your card can run a 7B model but could only train something around a few hundred million parameters from scratch. Tricks such as gradient checkpointing (recomputing activations instead of storing them), 8-bit optimisers, and LoRA (Chapter 10) stretch these limits.

In plain words

Fewer bits per weight means a smaller, faster model with a little less accuracy. 16-bit is standard; 4-bit is the home-use sweet spot. To check whether a model fits, multiply its parameters by the bytes per weight and add a gigabyte or two for context and overhead.
